Forms are everywhere. From signing up for a newsletter to applying for benefits, they're often the most critical point of interaction between a user and a service. Unfortunately, they're also one of the most common sources of frustration, especially for people with disabilities.

Designing accessible forms isn't just about compliance. It's about making sure everyone can complete essential tasks independently and with confidence.

**Use clear, visible labels**

Every input should have a label that stays visible. Placeholder text that disappears when someone starts typing can leave users unsure of what they're supposed to enter.

**Group related fields**

Use fieldsets and legends to group related questions, such as parts of an address. This helps screen reader users understand the structure of the form.

**Write helpful error messages**

Instead of "Invalid input," tell users exactly what went wrong and how to fix it. For example: "Enter a date in the format MM/DD/YYYY."

**Don't rely on color alone**

If you highlight errors in red, add an icon or text as well, so people with color blindness don't miss them.

**Test with real users**

Automated tools can catch many issues, but nothing replaces testing with people who use assistive technologies every day.

By following these principles, you can create forms that are not only accessible but also easier for everyone to use. After all, a better form means fewer errors, fewer abandoned applications, and happier users.
